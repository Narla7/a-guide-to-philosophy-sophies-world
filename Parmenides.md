# Parmenides
*Pre-Socratic natural philosopher — from Sophie's World, extreme-detail summary. Chapter context: first systematic philosophers ask — is there a basic substance everything is made of, and how can it change? From ~500 B.C. the Eleatics at Elea (Southern Italy) take up the Milesian puzzle: how can one single basic substance suddenly change into something else?*

The natural philosophers were the earliest Greek philosophers, each with his own "project" — what he was especially concerned to find out. They assumed "something" had always existed and asked what basic substance lies at the root of all change, seeking underlying laws by studying nature itself rather than myths. Only fragments survive, mostly via Aristotle two centuries later, who reports conclusions more than paths; what matters is the questions they asked and the type of answer they sought.

## Core thesis
Everything that exists **has always existed**; **nothing can come from nothing**, and nothing that exists can become nothing (the Greeks largely took everlastingness for granted; Parmenides radicalizes it). Further: **there is no such thing as actual change** — nothing can become anything other than it was. Parmenides of Elea (c. 540–480 B.C.).

## Arguments / reasoning (full detail, all examples from book)
- Admits the senses show constant flux, but the senses conflict with **reason**; forced to choose, he chooses **reason**.
- Against "I'll believe it when I see it": Parmenides doesn't believe even when he sees — the senses give an **incorrect picture**, a perceptual illusion; the philosopher's task is to expose it.
- Sophie's rehearsal (kept as philosophy): Parmenides was courageous to deny visible change; plain water never turns into more than ice/steam — not even a watermelon (watermelons contain more than water).

## Key terms
**rationalism** — unshakable faith in human reason as the primary source of world-knowledge; reason vs senses; permanence vs change; "nothing can come from nothing."

## Significance / legacy
Makes reason the criterion even against observation; sets the puzzle the next thinkers (Heraclitus as opposite, Empedocles/Anaxagoras/Democritus as reconcilers) must solve.

## Quotes
- "...nothing can come from nothing..."
