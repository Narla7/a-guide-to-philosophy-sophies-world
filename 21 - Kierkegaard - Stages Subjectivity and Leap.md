# 21 - Kierkegaard - Stages, Subjectivity and Leap

*Context: Gaarder presents Kierkegaard (1813–1855) as Hegel's Danish individualist antidote — the blue-bottle corrective to red-bottle World Spirit.*

## 1. The Red Bottle vs. The Blue Bottle — framing device

Gaarder introduces Kierkegaard through Alice's two bottles:

- Red drink = Pantheism / Idealism / Romantics' world spirit / Hegel: everything merges into one person, one thought, one ego, one world reason. Contrasts erased, individual differences erased.
- Blue drink = Individualism: woods slide apart, every tree a world in itself, lake becomes boundless ocean in detail, sparrows, spider, plant louse, ants each moving legs in own manner, Alberto becomes wondrous stranger, Sophie becomes uniquely Sophie Amundsen.

Alberto's verdict:

> "Both the red and the blue, Sophie. We cannot say the Romantics were wrong in holding that there is only one reality. But maybe they were a little bit narrow in their outlook."

> "He certainly had a sharp eye for the significance of the individual. We are more than 'children of our time.' And moreover, every single one of us is a unique individual who only lives once."

Leibniz is named as earlier parallel: Leibniz reacted against Spinoza as Kierkegaard reacted against Hegel. Hans Christian Andersen is named as contemporary Danish fellow-eye for detail.

Core charge:

> "He was more interested in the broad scope of history. This was just what made Kierkegaard so indignant. He thought that both the idealism of the Romantics and Hegel's 'historicism' had obscured the individual's responsibility for his own life. Therefore to Kierkegaard, Hegel and the Romantics were tarred with the same brush."

## 2. Life

- Born 1813, subjected to very severe upbringing by his father. Religious melancholia as legacy.
- Broke off engagement in 1841. Copenhagen bourgeoisie did not look kindly on it. Became outcast, object of scorn.
- Learned to give as good as he got; became what Ibsen later called "an enemy of the people."
- Not only engagement: toward end of life aggressively critical of society.
- Famous line: "The whole of Europe is on the road to bankruptcy." Believed he lived in age utterly devoid of passion and commitment.
- Particularly incensed by vapidness of established Danish Lutheran Church. Merciless criticism of "Sunday Christianity" — Gaarder glosses via Sophie as today's "confirmation Christianity" where kids get confirmed only for presents.

Christianity as either/or, not more-or-less:

> "To Kierkegaard, Christianity was both so overwhelming and so irrational that it had to be an either/or. It was not good being 'rather' or 'to some extent' religious. Because either Jesus rose on Easter Day--or he did not. And if he really did rise from the dead, if he really died for our sake--then this is so overwhelming that it must permeate our entire life."

> "To Kierkegaard, religion and knowledge were like fire and water. It was not enough to believe that Christianity is 'true.' Having a Christian faith meant following a Christian way of life."

## 3. Intellectual path: Socrates, Berlin, Hegel

- Began theology at 17, increasingly absorbed in philosophical questions.
- Age 27 took master's degree with dissertation *On the Concept of Irony*. Battled Romantic irony and Romantics' uncommitted play with illusion. Posited "Socratic irony" in contrast.
- Even though Socrates used irony, purpose was eliciting fundamental truths about life. Unlike Romantics, Socrates was what Kierkegaard called an "existential" thinker: thinker who draws his entire existence into his philosophical reflection.
- After breaking engagement 1841, went to Berlin, attended Schelling's lectures.
- Did not meet Hegel — Hegel died ten years earlier — but Hegel's system predominant in Berlin and Europe, used as all-purpose explanation for every question.
- Kierkegaard's claim: Hegelian "objective truths" totally irrelevant to personal life of individual.

Famous sarcasm about Hegelian professor:

> "While the ponderous Sir Professor explains the entire mystery of life, he has in distraction forgotten his own name; that he is a man, neither more nor less, not a fantastic three-eighths of a paragraph."

> "Kierkegaard thought Hegel had forgotten that he was a man."

## 4. Existence and "the truth for me"

- Question "what is a man?" cannot be answered in general terms. Broad description of human nature totally without interest.
- Only important thing was each man's "own existence." You don't experience own existence behind desk. Only when we act — especially when we make significant choices — that we relate to own existence.
- Buddha story as illustration (Gaarder says Buddha's philosophy also took man's existence as starting point):
  - Monk asks Buddha for clearer answers to fundamental questions on what world is and what man is.
  - Buddha likens monk to man pierced by poisoned arrow. Wounded man has no theoretical interest in what arrow made of, what poison dipped in, which direction came from. Wants someone to pull it out and treat wound.
  - That is existentially important.
- Both Buddha and Kierkegaard had strong sense of only existing for brief moment. Then you don't sit behind desk and philosophize about world spirit.

Formula:

> "According to Kierkegaard, rather than searching for the Truth with a capital T, it is more important to find the kind of truths that are meaningful to the individual's life. It is important to find 'the truth for me.' He thus sets the individual, or each and every man, up against the 'system.'"

## 5. Truth as subjectivity, faith, passion

- "Truth is 'subjective.'" Does *not* mean it doesn't matter what we think. Means really important truths are personal. Only these truths are "true for me."
- Example: whether Christianity is true. Not question one can relate to theoretically/academically. For person who "understands himself in life," question of life and death. Not something to discuss for discussion's sake. To be approached with greatest passion and sincerity.
- Falling-into-water analogy: if you fall into water, you have no theoretical interest in whether you will drown. Neither "interesting" nor "uninteresting" whether alligators in water. Question of life or death.

Distinction developed:

> "So we must therefore distinguish between the philosophical question of whether God exists and the individual's relationship to the same question, a situation in which each and every man is utterly alone. Fundamental questions such as these can only be approached through faith. Things we can know through reason, or knowledge, are according to Kierkegaard totally unimportant."

- Reasoned truth example: 8 + 4 = 12. Absolutely certain. Type every philosopher since Descartes talked about. But do we include it in daily prayers? Ponder over when dying? Not at all. Objective and general, but totally immaterial to each man's existence.
- Faith examples: you can never know whether person forgives you when you wrong them — therefore existentially important. Nor know whether person loves you — just have to believe or hope. More important than sum of angles in triangle = 180 degrees. You don't think about law of cause and effect or modes of perception in middle of first kiss.

Central quote on faith and objective uncertainty:

> "If I am capable of grasping God objectively, I do not believe, but precisely because I cannot do this I must believe. If I wish to preserve myself in faith I must constantly be intent upon holding fast the objective uncertainty, so as to remain out upon the deep, over seventy thousand fathoms of water, still preserving my faith."

- Many previously tried to prove existence of God or bring him within bounds of rationality. If you content yourself with proof/logical argument, you suffer loss of faith, and with it loss of religious passion.
- What matters is not whether Christianity is true, but whether it is true *for you*.
- Same thought expressed in Middle Ages maxim: *credo quia absurdum* — rendered in text as "I believe because it is irrational." If Christianity had appealed to reason, and not to other sides of us, it would not be question of faith.

## 6. Social criticism: the public, the crowd

- Three concepts — existential, subjective truth, faith — formulated as criticism of philosophical tradition and Hegel, but also trenchant social criticism.
- Individual in modern urban society had become "the public." Predominant characteristic of crowd/masses was noncommittal "talk." Gaarder updates: today we would say "conformity" — everybody "thinks" and "believes in" same things without deeper feeling.
- Sharp pen, bitter irony. Sayings:
  - "the crowd is the untruth"
  - "the truth is always in the minority"
  - most people had superficial approach to life.

## 7. The three stages on life's way — in full

Term "stages" emphasizes one can live at one of two lower stages and then suddenly leap to higher stage. Many live at same stage all life.

### 7.1 Aesthetic stage

> "He who lives at the aesthetic stage lives for the moment and grasps every opportunity of enjoyment. Good is whatever is beautiful, satisfying, or pleasant. This person lives wholly in the world of the senses, and is a slave to his own desires and moods. Everything that is boring is bad."

Expansions in Gaarder:

- Typical Romantic is also typical aesthete — more than pure sensory enjoyment.
- Person who has reflective approach to reality — or to art or philosophy engaged in — is living at aesthetic stage.
- Even possible to have aesthetic/reflective attitude to sorrow and suffering. Then vanity has taken over.
- Portrait: Ibsen's Peer Gynt.

Angst mechanism:

> "A person who lives at the aesthetic stage can easily experience angst, or a sense of dread, and a feeling of emptiness. If this happens, there is also hope. According to Kierkegaard, angst is almost positive. It is an expression of the fact that the individual is in an 'existential situation,' and can now elect to make the great leap to a higher stage. But it either happens or it doesn't. It doesn't help to be on the verge of making the leap if you don't do it completely. It is a matter of 'either/or.' But nobody can do it for you. It is your own choice."

Gaarder compares to deciding to quit drinking or drugs.

Category of decision reminiscent of Socrates: all true insight comes from within. Choice leading to leap from aesthetic to ethical or religious must come from within. Ibsen depicts in Peer Gynt. Another masterly description: Dostoevsky's *Crime and Punishment* — how existential choice springs from inner need and despair.

### 7.2 Ethical stage

> "And so perhaps you will begin to live at the ethical stage. This is characterized by seriousness and consistency of moral choices."

- Not unlike Kant's ethics of duty. Try to live by law of morals.
- Like Kant, drew attention first and foremost to human temperament. Important thing is not what you think precisely right or wrong. What matters is you choose to have opinion at all on right or wrong.
- Aesthete's only concern is whether something is fun or boring.
- Risk: becoming too serious. Kierkegaard never claimed ethical stage satisfactory. Even dutiful person eventually tired of always being dedicated and meticulous. Lots experience fatigue late in life. Some relapse into reflective life of aesthetic stage.

### 7.3 Religious stage

> "But others make a new leap to the religious stage. They take the 'jump into the abyss' of Faith's 'seventy thousand fathoms.' They choose faith in preference to aesthetic pleasure and reason's call of duty. And although it can be 'terrible to jump into the open arms of the living God,' as Kierkegaard put it, it is the only path to redemption."

- To Kierkegaard, religious stage was Christianity.
- Also became significant to non-Christian thinkers. Existentialism, inspired by Danish philosopher, flourished widely in twentieth century.

## 8. Note on scope — what Gaarder does NOT develop in this extraction

The assignment brief asked for Don Juan, Abraham/leap of faith as Abraham, sin/despair systematics, Christianity as paradox/offense. Those proper names/terms do **not** occur in Gaarder's Kierkegaard chapter in lines 17239–17941 as extracted:

- No Don Juan example; Gaarder's aesthetic portrait is Peer Gynt.
- No Abraham/Isaac/Fear and Trembling example; Gaarder's leap is rendered abstractly as "jump into abyss," "seventy thousand fathoms," "open arms of living God," plus Dostoevsky/Raskolnikov as literary parallel.
- Sin/despair as doctrines (*The Concept of Dread*, *The Sickness unto Death*) not expounded; only angst/dread/emptiness as trigger and despair as inner need are mentioned.
- Paradox/offense vocabulary not used; same idea is rendered as either/or, fire vs water, *credo quia absurdum*.
- This summary therefore stays faithful to Gaarder's actual examples rather than importing standard Kierkegaard lore from outside the book.
