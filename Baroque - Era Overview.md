# Baroque - Era Overview

*Context: The Baroque 17th century — illusion, war and ephemerality as the stage for the new mechanistic physics from Copernicus to Newton.*

## 1. The Baroque Era – Spirit of the Age

**Thesis:** Baroque holds irreconcilable contrasts together — splendor and transience, optimism and seclusion, illusion and exposure.

**Arguments / Details:**
- **Name:** "Baroque" originally meant an irregular pearl; art of the period was rich in contrasting, irregular, pompous forms, unlike the harmonious, plain Renaissance.
- **Central tension:** irreconcilable contrasts held together:
  - Renaissance optimism vs. religious seclusion, self-denial, monastic withdrawal.
  - Proud palaces vs. remote monasteries; luxury vs. vanity about transience.
- **Mottoes:**
  - *carpe diem* – "seize the day."
  - *memento mori* – "remember that you must die." Baroque paintings show luxurious lifestyle with a small skull in a corner.
  - Preoccupation with ephemerality: all beauty perishes; nothing lasts.
- **Politics and class:**
  - Thirty Years' War 1618–1648 ravaged Europe, especially Germany; wars of Protestant vs. Catholic but also power politics; France emerges dominant.
  - Enormous class difference: French aristocracy / Court of Versailles vs. poverty of people. Magnificence presupposes power.
  - Intrigue, plotting, assassinations; art / architecture full of ornate nooks and crannies mirrors political intrigue. Example: Gustav III of Sweden murdered at masked ball at Opera 1792 – baroque circumstances, end of Swedish Baroque; his "enlightened despotism" parallels Louis XIV ~100 years earlier, vain, French ceremony, lover of theater.

**Terms:** Baroque (irregular pearl); *carpe diem*; *memento mori*; ephemerality.

## 2. Life as Theater / Life as Dream

**Thesis:** Baroque theater builds illusion only to expose it — a moral mirror of human frailty and dream-uncertainty.

**Arguments / Details:**
- Modern theater born with scenery, machinery; builds illusion on stage only to expose that the play is illusion – reflection of human life.
- Theater as moral mirror: "pride comes before a fall," merciless portrait of human frailty.
- Obsessive saying: "Life is a theater." Shakespeare (one foot Renaissance ~1600, one foot Baroque):
  - *As You Like It:* "All the world's a stage, And all the men and women merely players: They have their exits and their entrances; And one man in his time plays many parts."
  - *Macbeth:* "Life's but a walking shadow, a poor player That struts and frets his hour upon the stage, And then is heard no more; it is a tale Told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, Signifying nothing."
  - *Hamlet:* "To be or not to be – that is the question." One day walking earth, next day dead.
  - *The Tempest:* "We are such stuff as dreams are made on, and our little life is rounded with a sleep..." – chapter epigraph.
- Calderón de la Barca (b.1600), *Life Is a Dream:* "What is life? A madness. What is life? An illusion, a shadow, a story, and the greatest good is little enough, for all life is a dream..."
- Holberg's *Jeppe on the Mount* (Scandinavian transition Baroque→Enlightenment): Jeppe falls asleep in ditch, wakes in Baron's bed, thinks peasant life was dream; carried back, wakes again, thinks Baron's bed was dream. Borrowed from Calderón, who borrowed from *A Thousand and One Nights.*
- Deeper roots: Chuang-tzu: "Once I dreamed I was a butterfly, and now I no longer know whether I am Chuang-tzu, who dreamed I was a butterfly, or whether I am a butterfly dreaming that I am Chuang-tzu." Impossible to prove either way.
- Norwegian Baroque poet Petter Dass (1647–1707): describes here-and-now rural Northern Norway – lumpfish, cod, coal-fish – in same hymn as eternal God: "God is God if every land was waste, God is God if every man were dead." Earthly / here-and-now + celestial / hereafter together, reminiscent of Plato's senses vs. ideas.

**Terms:** theater-illusion; dream argument background; Shakespeare / Calderón / Holberg / Chuang-tzu / Petter Dass.

## 3. New Cosmology and the Mechanistic Worldview (full Renaissance physics background as Baroque's condition)

**Thesis:** From Copernicus to Newton, one universal mathematical law replaces two worlds, producing the mechanistic worldview and determinism.

**Arguments / Details:**
- **Copernicus (1543, *On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres*):** not sun round earth but earth round sun; day / night because earth turns on axis; heliocentric = everything centers on sun. Easier to explain observations. Limits: still made sun center of universe (today: sun one of infinite stars, our stars one of billions of galaxies); still held circular orbits from Platonic prejudice that sphere / circle is most perfect heavenly figure.
- **Kepler (early 1600s, German):** from comprehensive observations: planets move in elliptical (oval) orbits with sun at one focus; speed greatest when closest to sun; farther orbit = slower. First to state earth is a planet like others. Same physical laws everywhere in universe. Also: force holding planets (solar force), tides from lunar force.
- **Galileo (contemporary of Kepler):** telescope – moon's craters, mountains / valleys like earth; Jupiter's four moons – earth not alone in having moon. Most important: **Law of Inertia:** "A body remains in the state which it is in, at rest or in motion, as long as no external force compels it to change its state."
  - Refutes ancient objection to moving earth (hurled stone would fall yards away): like apple dropped in moving train falls straight down – retains speed by inertia.
  - Inclined-plane experiments: weight = gravity / gravitation as motive force; rolling marble across sloping board traces curved parabola – two forces simultaneously (forward motion + downward pull). Same for cannonball: propelled forward yet drawn to earth, contra Aristotle (gentle curve then vertical fall). Demonstrated, not speculated.
  - Error: mocked Kepler, rejected action at distance – denied moon rules tides, denied gravitation between heavenly bodies, though obsessed with earthly gravity.
- **Newton (1642–1727):** final description + explanation of solar system, using Galileo's dynamics:
  - **Law of Universal Gravitation:** every object attracts every other with force increasing with size / mass and decreasing with distance. E.g. two elephants attract more than two mice; two elephants in same zoo more than Indian elephant in India + African elephant in Africa.
  - Universal: operative everywhere, also between heavenly bodies. Apple-tree legend: same force pulling apple pulls moon – why moon orbits earth eternally instead of crashing? Two simultaneous constant forces: (1) rectilinear outward motion from solar system's formation, persisting in vacuum without resistance (inertia: "A body remains in its state of rest or rectilinear motion until compelled to change by impressed force"); (2) inward fall toward earth / sun by gravitation. Result: elliptical orbit. Planets round sun = original straight motion + sunward gravitation.
  - Abolishes medieval two-sets-of-laws (heaven vs. earth); same laws everywhere; heliocentric view confirmed and explained.
  - Existential shock: man on random planet in vast galaxy, special status lost (parallel later to Darwin); Church resistance. Where is God if earth not center, God not "upstairs"? Newton himself unshaken – natural laws prove great almighty God. Compensation idea: no absolute center → as many centers as people; each person center of a universe.

**Terms:** heliocentrism; elliptical orbits; Law of Inertia; Law of Universal Gravitation; abolition of two laws.

## 4. Philosophical Consequence — Materialism vs. Idealism, Mechanism, Determinism

**Thesis:** New science nourishes both materialism and idealism at once; mechanism culminates in man-machine and Laplacean determinism, met by indivisibility objections.

**Arguments / Details:**
- Both present simultaneously as never before. Idealism = what exists is at bottom spiritual. Materialism = all derives from concrete matter; nourished by new science.
- Hobbes (English): all phenomena including man / animals exclusively particles of matter; consciousness / soul = motion of tiny particles in brain – agrees with Democritus 2000 years earlier.
- Newton: same laws of motion for whole universe; all natural change explainable by universal gravitation + motion; in principle calculable with mathematical precision → **mechanistic worldview.** World as one big machine (*mechane* = machine in Greek). Hobbes and Newton see no contradiction with belief in God.
- Later radicalization: La Mettrie, *L'homme machine* ("Man-the-machine"): leg has muscles to walk, brain has "muscles" to think. Laplace: if intelligence knew position of all particles at a time, "nothing would be unknown, future and past open" → **determinism:** everything predetermined, "written in stars," no free will; thoughts / dreams also mechanical products. 19th-century German materialists: thought is to brain as urine to kidneys, gall to liver.
- Counter-story: Russian astronaut ("been in space, never saw God / angels") vs. Russian brain surgeon Christian ("operated clever brains, never saw a thought") – thoughts not operable, not decomposable into parts; delusion cannot be excised. Leibniz: material divisible into ever smaller bits, soul indivisible even into two. Scalpel for soul?

**Terms:** materialism vs. idealism; mechanistic worldview (*mechane*); determinism (Laplace); man-machine (La Mettrie); Hobbes; Leibniz on indivisibility.

**Legacy:** sets the stage for [[Descartes]] — certainty and mind–body problems dominate next 150 years.
