# 02 - Myths - Mythological World Picture

## Chapter context
Before the first philosophers (c. 600 B.C.), all answers came from religion handed down as myth; the book uses Norse myths as the lived example.

## 1. What a myth is
- Definition: a story about the gods that sets out to explain **why life is as it is**.
- Philosophy, by the book's definition, is the **completely new way of thinking that evolved in Greece about 600 years before Christ**: replacing mythological explanations with natural ones.
- Over millennia a "wild profusion" of mythological explanations of philosophical questions spread worldwide; Greek philosophers attempted to prove they were not to be trusted.

## 2. Thor as worked example: thunder, rain, fertility
- Thor rides the sky in a chariot drawn by two goats; swings his hammer → thunder and lightning.
- Etymology given: Norwegian "Thor-døn" = Thor's roar; Swedish "aska," originally "as-aka" = "god's journey" over the heavens.
- When thunder/lightning comes, rain comes; rain was vital to Viking farmers. Hence Thor worshipped as **god of fertility**.
- Mythological explanation chain: Thor swings hammer → rain → corn germinates and thrives. How plants grow/yield crops was not understood, but rain was clearly connected, and rain was Thor — so Thor among the most important Norse gods.

## 3. The cosmic order: Midgard, Asgard, Utgard — good vs evil
- Vikings: inhabited world is an island under constant outside threat = **Midgard** (kingdom in the middle); within it lies **Asgard**, domain of the gods.
- Outside Midgard: **Utgard**, domain of treacherous giants using cunning tricks to destroy the world — the **"forces of chaos."**
- Nearly universal pattern: a **precarious balance between the forces of good and evil**, in Norse and almost all cultures.
- Concrete stake: giants may abduct **Freyja, goddess of fertility** — then nothing grows, women bear no children. So giants must be held in check.
- Thor's hammer is more than rain-maker: key weapon against chaos, almost unlimited power — hurled at giants to slay them, always returns like a boomerang. This is the mythological explanation for **how nature's balance is maintained and why good/evil constantly struggle** — precisely what philosophers rejected.

## 4. Myth demands action: rites and offerings
- Mortals cannot idly wait for gods during drought/plague; they must act in the struggle against evil via **religious ceremonies / rites**.
- Chief Norse rite: the **offering** — sacrificing to a god increases that god's power to conquer chaos. Example: goat to Thor; to Odin, sometimes **human sacrifices**.

## 5. Case study: "The Lay of Thrym" (Elder Edda)
- Thor wakes, hammer gone; hands tremble, beard shakes. With henchman **Loki** he asks **Freyja** to lend her wings so Loki can fly to **Jotunheim**, land of giants, to investigate.
- Giant king **Thrym** boasts he hid the hammer **seven leagues under the earth** and will return it only if given Freyja as bride.
- Book's gloss: a full-blown **hostage incident** — giants hold the gods' vital defensive weapon (total control over gods and mortals if kept); demanding Freyja is equally unacceptable (loss of fertility goddess = grass gone, gods and mortals die). Deadlock.
- Loki reports back; Freyja must don wedding attire — she refuses furiously ("man-crazy" to marry a giant).
- **Heimdall's** plan: Thor dresses as bride — hair up, two stones under tunic as breasts. Thor unenthusiastic but accepts as only way to recover hammer. Thor as bride + Loki as bridesmaid = the gods' **"anti-terrorist squad"** breaching the stronghold (book's modern terms; also "Batman or James Bond of the gods").
- At the feast the "bride" devours an entire ox, eight salmon, three barrels of beer — Thrym astonished, near-exposure; Loki covers: Freyja hasn't eaten for a week from longing for Jotunheim.
- Thrym lifts veil to kiss bride, sees Thor's burning eyes; Loki covers again: bride hasn't slept for a week from wedding excitement. Thrym orders hammer laid in bride's lap per ceremony.
- Thor roars with laughter on receiving it, kills Thrym, wipes out giants and kin — gruesome hostage affair with happy ending; forces of evil conquered again.

## 6. What myths really do: explanation + enacted magic
- Not mere entertainment; tries to explain something. Possible interpretation offered:
  - Drought → "giants stole Thor's hammer?"
  - Changing seasons → winter = Nature dies because hammer is in Jotunheim; spring = Thor wins it back.
- Myth is not only explanation: people perform **religious ceremonies tied to myths** — e.g., enact the drama (villager dresses as bride with stones for breasts) to **make it rain** and crops grow. Many worldwide examples of dramatizing seasonal myths to "speed up nature's processes."

## 7. From Norse to Greek myths — and the first critique
- Countless myths of Thor, Odin, Freyr and Freya, Hoder, Balder and others; such mythological notions flourished worldwide until philosophers "tampered" with them.
- Greece had the same structure: Zeus, Apollo, Hera, Athene, Dionysos, Asclepios, Heracles, Hephaestos, etc., handed down for centuries.
- Turning point ~700 B.C.: **Homer and Hesiod write the myths down** — new situation: once in written form, they can be **discussed/criticized**.
- Earliest philosophers criticized Homer's gods for resembling mortals too much — egoistic, treacherous. First claim: **myths are nothing but human notions.**
- **Xenophanes (c. 570 B.C.)** as exponent: men created gods in their own image — born, with bodies, clothes, language like ours. Ethiopians depict gods black and flat-nosed, Thracians blue-eyed and fair-haired. **If oxen, horses, and lions could draw, they would depict gods like oxen, horses, lions.**

## 8. Social conditions for philosophy: the city-state
- Greeks found many **city-states** in Greece, Southern Italy, Asia Minor, where slaves did manual work, freeing citizens for politics and culture.
- In that environment anyone could question how society ought to be organized **on his own behalf** — and thus ask philosophical questions **without recourse to ancient myths**.
- Book's formula: development **from a mythological mode of thought to one based on experience and reason**. Aim of early philosophers: **natural, not supernatural, explanations for natural processes** — understand processes by studying **nature itself**, not god-stories.

## Key terms
Myth; Thor's hammer; Midgard / Asgard / Utgard-Jotunheim; Freyja; forces of chaos vs precarious balance; offering/rite; "Lay of Thrym"; Homer/Hesiod; Xenophanes' anthropomorphism critique.

## Significance / legacy
- Myth is presented as proto-answer to the same questions philosophy asks (rain, crops, seasons, evil) plus a call to ritual action; philosophy keeps the questions, rejects the method.
- Xenophanes' critique is the book's first explicit philosophical argument: a projection theory of religion.
