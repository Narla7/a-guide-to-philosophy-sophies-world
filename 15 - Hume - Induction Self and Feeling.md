# 15 - Hume - Induction, Self and Feeling

*Context: Sophie's empiricism lessons turn to David Hume, the most radical British empiricist.*

## 1. Life and place

- **David Hume (1711–1776)**, grew up near Edinburgh, Scotland.
- Family wanted him to take up law; he felt "an insurmountable resistance to everything but philosophy and learning."
- Lived in the Age of Enlightenment at same time as Voltaire and Rousseau; traveled widely in Europe, settled in Edinburgh toward end of life.
- Main work **A Treatise of Human Nature**, published when he was 28, but he claimed he got the idea at 15.
- Presented as most important of the empiricists, and as the one who set Immanuel Kant on the road to his philosophy.
- More than any other philosopher, he takes everyday world / child's experience as starting point.

## 2. Program: clean up concepts, return to experience

- Mission: clean up woolly concepts and thought-constructions from Middle Ages and 17th-century rationalism.
- Return to spontaneous experience of the world.
- Quote: No philosopher "will ever be able to take us behind the daily experiences or give us rules of conduct that are different from those we get through reflections on everyday life."
- Famous fork / flames test: "If we take in our hands any volume ... let us ask, 'Does it contain any abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number?' No. 'Does it contain any experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence?' No. Commit it then to the flames, for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion."
- Sophie dialogue frames this as tidying thoughts and book collections.

## 3. Impressions vs ideas; simple vs complex

- Man has two types of perceptions:
  - **Impressions**: immediate sensation of external reality — stronger, livelier, original.
  - **Ideas**: recollection / reflection of impressions — pale imitation stored in mind. Impression is direct cause of idea.
- Example: burning yourself on hot oven = impression; afterward recalling it = idea.
- Both impressions and ideas can be **simple or complex**:
  - Direct experience of an apple (Locke example recalled) = complex impression.
- Mind never invents: it only cuts out and pastes together what once entered as simple impressions.
- **Angels**: complex idea = human figure (seen) + wings (seen, but not on human). Two unrelated experiences associated in imagination. False idea, must be rejected.
- Same for Pegasus / winged horse, "crocophants" (earlier example).
- Critical method: for every idea ask: **From which impression does this idea originate?** Break complex into single ideas, then check for corresponding simple impressions.
  - Person who never saw gold can never visualize streets of gold.

## 4. Heaven and God as cut-and-paste ideas

- Against Descartes's "clear and distinct" guarantee:
- **Heaven / New Jerusalem**: compounded of pearly gates, streets of gold, angels by the score. Each is itself complex. Break down to simples: pearl, gate, street, gold, white-robed figure, wings. We had simples, but pasted them.
- **God as infinitely intelligent, wise, good being**: complex idea = intelligence + wisdom + goodness, infinitely enlarged. If we never knew those qualities, never such idea.
- **God as severe but just Father**: severity + justice + father. Critics since Hume link "heavenly father" to experience of own father as little child.
- General rule: oppose all thoughts that cannot be traced back to sense perceptions; want to "dismiss all this meaningless nonsense which long has dominated metaphysical thought and brought it into disrepute."

## 5. No unalterable ego: bundle theory

- Target: Descartes's ego / "I" as foundation, the one clear distinct perception.
- Apply Hume's method: is ego single or complex?
  - Sophie admits: volatile, changeable mind, liking/disliking same people — complex.
- Is there corresponding **complex impression** of ego experienced simultaneously?
  - No: not same at 4 as at 15; temperament and self-image alter minute to minute; can suddenly feel like "new person."
  - Feeling of unalterable ego is false perception.
- Formulation quoted: perception of ego is "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed one another with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement."
- Mind is "a kind of theater, where several perceptions successively make their appearance; pass, re-pass, slide away, and mingle in an infinite variety of postures and situations."
- No underlying personal identity beneath/behind perceptions.
- Analogy: movie screen — images change so rapidly we don't register film is single pictures; in reality pictures not connected; film is collection of instants.
- Parallel explicitly drawn to **Buddha, ~2,500 years earlier**: life as unbroken succession of mental/physical processes; infant not same as adult; "nothing of which I can say 'this is mine,' and nothing of which I can say 'this is me'"; no I / unalterable ego. Buddha's last words: "Decay is inherent in all compound things. Work out your own salvation with diligence" — Hume/Democritus could have said same.

## 6. Soul, God, agnosticism, miracles

- Rejects rationalist assumption of eternal soul following from unalterable ego.
- Rejects attempts to prove immortality of soul or existence of God by reason as "rationalistic claptrap." Does not rule them out.
- Not Christian, not confirmed atheist: **agnostic** — existence of God can neither be proved nor disproved.
- Dying anecdote: asked if believed in life after death: "It is also possible that a knob of coal placed upon the fire will not burn" — unconditional open-mindedness, accepts only what senses give, holds other possibilities open.
- Faith and miracles are matters of faith, not knowledge/reason. With Hume final link between faith and knowledge broken.
- Miracles: people need "supernatural" happenings; alleged miracles always far distant place / long ago. Hume rejects only because never experienced any — but never experienced they *couldn't* happen either.
- Miracle defined as against laws of nature, but meaningless to allege we experienced laws of nature. We experience stone falling when let go; if it didn't fall, then we experienced that.
- No two natures (natural vs supernatural) — that would be back to claptrap.

## 7. Causality critique in full: habit, constant conjunction, problem of induction

- Core question: how can Sophie be certain stone **will always** fall?
  - She: seen it so many times, absolutely certain.
  - Hume: you experienced stone falling many times, never experienced it *will always* fall. Usual to say "law of gravitation," but never experienced such law. Only experienced things fall.
- Expectation of one thing following another does not lie in things themselves but in our mind; associated with **habit/custom**.
- Concept of "unbreakable laws of nature" arises because we are so used to one thing following other that we expect same every time.
- Hume probably as convinced as Sophie stone will fall, but points out he has not experienced *why*.
- **Child verification**: who more surprised if stone floated hour or two — adult or one-year-old? Adult, because adult knows how "unnatural" it is; child hasn't learned how nature behaves / nature hasn't become habit. Child not yet slave of expectations of habit, thus more open-minded, greater philosopher: comes without preconceived opinions, perceives world as it is without putting more into things than experienced.
- **Magic show exercise**: adult has most fun because knows how impossible it is; child for whom laws not yet learned gets no fun from defiance.
- **Law of causation**: everything must have cause. Example: **black billiard ball rolled against white at rest** — white starts to move. Usual: impact is cause. But strictly: only experienced white ball begins to roll; experienced one event after other, not that other happens *because of* first. Never experienced actual cause.
- Child would not stare if both remained motionless after impact.
- When we speak of "laws of nature" or "cause and effect," we speak of what we **expect**, rather than what is "reasonable." Laws neither reasonable nor unreasonable, simply are. Expectation white ball moves is not innate; not born with expectations; world is like it is, something we get to know.
- Hume does not deny unbreakable natural laws exist, but since we cannot experience laws themselves we easily jump to conclusions.

### 7.1 False causes and science

- **Black horses / black crows**: seeing whole herd black horses doesn't mean all horses black; seeing only black crows doesn't mean no white crow. Philosopher/scientist must not reject possibility of white crow; hunting "white crow" is science's principal task.
- **Lightning/thunder**: many imagine lightning causes thunder because thunder comes after. Really not so different from billiard balls. Both happen (nearly) together due to third factor: electric discharge causes both.
- **Bertrand Russell's chicken** (modern empiricist example used): chicken fed daily when farmer's wife comes concludes causal link between approach and feed — until one day she comes and wrings its neck. One thing following another does not mean causal link.
- **Superstition**: black cat crosses street, later fall break arm — no causal link. Philosophy warns against jumping to conclusions.
- **Science / medicine**: many get well after drug doesn't mean drug cured them. Need large control group who think given same medicine but given flour and water. If they also get well, third factor — e.g., belief medicine works — cured them.

## 8. Ethics: feelings over reason; is-ought

- Rebels against rationalists (Socrates to Locke) holding ability to distinguish right/wrong inherent in reason / natural right.
- Thesis: it is **sentiments/feelings**, not reason, that determines what we say/do.
  - Help someone in need because of feelings, not reason. Not bothering to help also feelings. Neither reasonable nor unreasonable not to help, but could be unkind.
  - Everybody has feeling for others' welfare, capacity for compassion — nothing to do with reason.
- Killing example: not always unwise to get rid of person to achieve something — can be good idea instrumentally. Protest "that person wants to live too, therefore you ought not kill" is moving from descriptive **is** ("wants to live") to normative **ought** ("ought not kill") — from reason's view nonsense, like "lots cheat taxes, therefore I ought to."
- **Hume's law**: you can never draw conclusions from is-sentences to ought-sentences. Yet exceedingly common in newspapers, party programs, speeches:
  - "More want air travel. Therefore more airports ought to be built" — nonsense, must think environment, build railroads.
  - "New oilfields raise living standards 10%. Therefore develop rapidly" — no, environment, Norway living standard high enough.
  - "Law passed by Senate, therefore all citizens ought abide" — frequently against deepest convictions.
- Conclusion: cannot use reason as yardstick for how ought act. Acting responsibly is deepening feelings for welfare of others, not strengthening reason.
- Quotes: "'Tis not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger."
- **Nazis**: murdered millions — wrong in emotional life, not reason. Many exceedingly clear-headed; ice-cold calculation behind callous decisions. Convicted as gruesome murderers, not for being "unreasonable." Unsound mind can acquit (not accountable), but never acquitted for being unfeeling.
- **Flood / overpopulation**: if callous, leave to "cold reason," might think quite in order millions die in overpopulated world. Anger at such thought comes not from reason but feeling.
