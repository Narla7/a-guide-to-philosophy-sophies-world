# 11 – Renaissance: Humanism, Science and Reformation

Context: Gaarder treats the Renaissance (late 14th–16th c., from Northern Italy northward) as Europe's "fifteenth birthday" — antiquity reborn after the medieval "schoolday" — in man, nature, method, cosmos, and religion.

Story in one line: Sophie follows Hermes to Alberto's attic museum-flat, where he teaches the Renaissance in costume.

## 1. Historical and material background

### 1.1 What "Renaissance" means
- French *renaissance* = rebirth: rebirth of antique art/culture.
- Also "Renaissance humanism": after Dark Ages when everything seen through divine light, everything revolves around man again.
- Motto: "Go to the source" — first antiquity; digging sculptures/scrolls, learning Greek fashionable.
- Pedagogical aim: *studia humanitatis* give "classical education," form human qualities. Saying: "Horses are born, but human beings are not born — they are formed." Education makes us human.

### 1.2 Three technical preconditions
- Compass → navigation → great voyages of discovery.
- Firearms → European military superiority over American/Asiatic cultures and internal European power factor.
- Printing press (incunabula = cradle-books, before 1500) → spreads humanist ideas; breaks Church's monopoly as sole disseminator of knowledge.
- Follow-on: new instruments thick and fast; telescope gives astronomy wholly new basis; long trajectory toward moon rockets — and toward Hiroshima/Chernobyl.

### 1.3 Economic and cultural conditions
- Shift from subsistence to monetary economy; late-medieval cities, trades, commerce in new goods, money and banking.
- Middle class arises with relative freedom from necessity; necessities buyable; diligence/imagination/ingenuity rewarded; new demands on individual.
- Parallel to Greek city-states 2,000 years earlier: philosophy breaks from peasant myth then; Renaissance burghers break from feudal lords and church power now.
- Greek culture rediscovered through closer contact with Arabs in Spain and Byzantine culture in East: the three antique streams rejoin in one river.

## 2. New view of mankind

### 2.1 From God-centered to man-centered
- Medieval point of departure always God; Renaissance humanists take man himself as departure — like Greek philosophers, hence "rebirth."
- But more individualist than antiquity: not only *human*, but *unique individual*. Can tip into unrestrained worship of genius.
- Ideal: Renaissance man — universal genius embracing art, science, life.
- Figures/quotes:
  - Marsilio Ficino: "Know thyself, O divine lineage in mortal guise!"
  - Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, *Oration on the Dignity of Man* — unthinkable in Middle Ages.
- Man considered infinitely great and valuable, against medieval stress on sinfulness.

### 2.2 Body, art, limitlessness
- Anatomy: dissection of the dead resumes to learn construction; serves medicine and art.
- Nude returns in art after 1,000 years of prudery; man bold enough to be himself; no longer shameful.
- Man does not exist purely for God's sake; may delight in life here and now, not merely prepare for hereafter; freedom brings limitless possibilities; aim to exceed all boundaries.
- Contrast with ancient humanism: antiquity stressed tranquility, moderation, restraint; Renaissance humanists often immoderate, as if world reawakened. Conscious of epoch — they invent "Middle Ages" for the centuries between antiquity and themselves.
- Concrete example: Rome, "city of cities"/"hub of universe," fallen to 17,000 inhabitants by 1417; humanists make restoration cultural duty, above all St. Peter's over Peter the Apostle's grave: begun 1506, 120 years building + 50 for square; >200 m long, 130 m high, >16,000 m²; neither moderate nor restrained. Art/architecture/literature/music/philosophy/science flourish as never before.

## 3. New view of nature: pantheism and its shadow

- Thesis: feeling at home in world → nature regarded as positive.
- Many hold God present in creation: if infinite, must be in everything. Called **pantheism**. Medieval barrier God/Creation dissolved; nature as divine, "God's blossoming."
- Church hostility: Giordano Bruno — God in nature plus infinite universe — burned at stake in Rome's Flower Market, 1600.
- Shadow side / "anti-humanism": authoritarian State/Church power persists. Witch trials, heretic burnings, magic/superstition, bloody religious wars, brutal conquest of America.
- Methodological moral: no epoch purely good/evil; good and evil are twin intertwined threads — true also of the new science.

## 4. New scientific method

### 4.1 Empiricism against authority and pure reason
- Investigate nature with own senses.
- From 14th century: warnings against blind faith in old authorities (doctrine, Aristotle's natural philosophy) and against solving problems by thought alone.
- Medieval overbelief in reason corrected: every inquiry into natural phenomena must rest on observation, experience, experiment. Called empirical method: base knowledge on own experience, not dusty parchments or imagination.
- Antiquity knew empirical science, but systematic experiment is new.

### 4.2 Mathematics, power, ambivalence
- Observations must be expressed in precise mathematics; apparatus then only mathematics and scales, no calculators/electronic scales.
- Galileo Galilei: "Measure what can be measured, and make measurable what cannot be measured"; "the book of nature is written in the language of mathematics."
- Sequence: new method → technical revolution → technical breakthrough → every later invention; man breaks from mere natural condition; nature no longer simply what man is part of.
- Francis Bacon: "Knowledge is power" — practical value of knowledge; man intervenes in and controls nature.
- Gaarder's balance sheet, good/evil intertwined:
  - Spinning jenny → unemployment; medicines → new diseases; efficient agriculture → environmental impoverishment; washing machine/refrigerator → pollution/industrial waste.
  - Today's ecological threat makes some see technology as perilous maladjustment beyond control; optimists say still in cradle/teething troubles, will learn control without destroying nature and ourselves.
  - Gaarder/Alberto: truth in both; must stop interference in some areas, can succeed in others; no way back; since Renaissance man is more than part of creation, forming nature in his image. "What a piece of work is man!"

## 5. New world picture: from geocentrism to universal gravitation

### 5.1 Geocentric starting point
- Medieval: earth motionless center; sun/moon/stars/planets orbit it. Called geocentric. Christianity's God ruling on high above luminaries reinforced it. No observations had yet sown doubt.

### 5.2 Copernicus (1473–1543): heliocentrism
- 1543, *On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres*, published on author's death day.
- Thesis: not sun round earth but earth round sun; possible from existing observations; belief sun orbits earth is appearance caused by earth's axial rotation. Assuming earth + planets circle sun makes observations far easier to understand.
- Called heliocentric (everything centers on sun).
- Corrections: main point (earth moves round sun) correct; but sun is not universe's center — sun only one of infinite stars; our stars only one of billions of galaxies. Nor are orbits circular: circles assumed only from ancient perfection of sphere/circle since Plato.

### 5.3 Kepler (1571–1630): ellipses and universal law
- Early 1600s, from comprehensive observations: planets move in elliptical/oval orbits with sun at one focus; speed greatest nearest sun; farther orbit → slower movement.
- First to state earth is a planet like others.
- Same physical laws everywhere in universe.
- How known: investigated planetary motions with own senses, not ancient superstition.
- Already posited attractive solar force holding planets, explaining distance–speed relation; also lunar force causing tides (ebb/flow).

### 5.4 Galileo (1564–1642): telescope and inertia
- Roughly contemporary with Kepler; telescope observations: moon's craters — mountains/valleys like earth's; Jupiter has four moons — earth not alone in having a moon.
- Greatest contribution: Law of Inertia: "A body remains in the state which it is in, at rest or in motion, as long as no external force compels it to change its state."
- Refutes ancient objection to earth's rotation (hurled stone would land yards away if earth spins fast).
- Illustrations: train — dropped apple falls straight down, retains train's speed; rolling ball keeps speed after release; stops only through other forces (rough floor, gravity).
- Inclined-plane experiment (board + inked green marble): marble rolls down by weight/gravity; rolled across slopes downward; ink trace is curved, part of circle but not quite — a **parabola**. When two forces act simultaneously, trajectory is parabolic.
- Application: cannonball propelled obliquely continues over earth yet drawn down, describing same parabola. Aristotle's view (gentle curve then vertical fall) thus experimentally refuted; unknowable until demonstrated.
- Gaarder calls this cosmically the most important discovery.
- Limitation: Galileo mocks Kepler's lunar-tide theory, rejecting action at distance and interplanetary gravitation, though preoccupied with terrestrial gravity and force-controlled motion.

### 5.5 Newton (1642–1727): synthesis
- Provides final description *and* explanation of solar system/planetary orbits, partly via "Galileo's dynamics."
- Law of Universal Gravitation: every object attracts every other with force increasing with size/mass and decreasing with distance. Sophie's paraphrase accepted: two elephants attract more than two mice; two elephants in same zoo more than Indian elephant in India and African elephant in Africa.
- Universality: gravitation operative everywhere, also between heavenly bodies. Apple-tree story: apple's fall and moon's orbiting same force? Moon drawn tremendously (think force lifting seas 1–2 m at tide).
- Sophie's objection: moon should then crash. Answer: 50% right/wrong; two constant simultaneous forces:
  - Original rectilinear/flung-outward motion from solar system's formation, persisting forever in resistanceless vacuum.
  - Inward attraction toward sun/earth by gravitation.
  - Result: elliptical orbit. Planets circle sun from initial straight motion + solar gravitation.
- Newton's two laws used: inertia ("body remains in rest or rectilinear motion until compelled by impressed force") + Galileo's composition of forces → elliptical path.
- Achievement: same laws of motion everywhere; abolishes medieval two-sets-of-laws (heaven vs. earth); heliocentrism finally confirmed *and* explained.

### 5.6 Human meaning
- Burden like Darwin later (man from animals): man loses special status; Church resists massively in both cases.
- Naive question: where is God if earth is random planet, God/planets no longer simply "upstairs"?
- Newton's own faith unshaken: natural laws prove great almighty God. Self-image fares worse: must live on random planet in vast galaxy — still not wholly accepted.
- Renaissance counter-thought: with no absolute cosmic center, as many centers as people — each person center of a universe. Decentering can thus recenter the individual.

## 6. New religiosity and the Reformation

### 6.1 Break of philosophy/science from theology
- After Aquinas, cracks in Christian unifying culture; philosophy/science detach from church theology; religion freer toward reason.
- Emphasis: God unknowable by rationalism; submit to will rather than grasp mystery. Opens way to new scientific method *and* new religious fervor → Renaissance + Reformation (15th–16th c.).

### 6.2 Individual piety and Scripture in the vernacular
- Personal relationship to God outweighs relation to church as organization (e.g. nightly prayer).
- Medieval Catholic backbone: Latin liturgy/ritual; Bible only in Latin, read only by priests/monks.
- Renaissance: Bible translated Hebrew/Greek → national languages. Central to Reformation.

### 6.3 Martin Luther
- Not sole reformer; others remain Catholic (e.g. Erasmus of Rotterdam).
- Indulgences: forgiveness not via church/priest intercession nor bought "letters of indulgence"; trade forbidden by Catholic Church from mid-16th century.
- Deeper program: distance from medieval-rooted customs/dogmas; return to early New Testament Christianity. "Scripture alone" — as humanists to ancient sources, Luther to Christian source.
- Translates Bible into German, founding German written language; vernacular over Latin is Renaissance trait; taught himself Greek at 35 for the labor.
- Every man reads Bible, in a sense his own priest: priests have no preferential standing before God; congregations keep priests for practical service, but forgiveness/redemption not through rituals. Redemption "free" through faith alone, from Bible reading.
- Anthropology: mankind totally depraved after Fall; justified only through God's grace. "For the wages of sin is death." Gloomy vs. humanists.
- Typical Renaissance man? Yes in individualism, personal God-relation, vernacular, ad fontes; No unlike Ficino/Leonardo da Vinci; opposed by humanists like Erasmus who find his man too negative.

### 6.4 Erasmus and the humanist quarrel
- Erasmus of Rotterdam: ecclesiastical reformer staying in Roman Catholic Church.
- Opposes Luther over view of man: humanist optimism vs. Lutheran depravity/grace.
