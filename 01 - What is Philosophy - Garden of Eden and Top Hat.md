# 01 - What is Philosophy - Garden of Eden and Top Hat

## Chapter context
Sophie's opening letters ask "Who are you?" and "Where does the world come from?" — the philosophy course answers by defining philosophy as wonder and the attempt to climb back up the rabbit's fur.

## 1. The two opening questions
- **"Who are you?"** — Not a request for a name (Sophie Amundsen / Lillemor thought-experiment: would she be someone else with a different name?). Points to the problem of personal identity: you did not choose yourself, your looks, or even to be a human being.
- **"Where does the world come from?"** — Leads to a regress dilemma laid out in the den scene:
  - If space always existed, can anything "always" have existed? Something in us protests.
  - If space came from something else, that something must come from something else — infinite deferral.
  - At some point something must have come from nothing — but that also seems impossible.
  - The school answer "God created the world" does not solve it: what about God himself? He cannot have created himself before he had a "self." Saying "God always existed" reintroduces the rejected "always existed" option.
- Significance: these two questions — human identity and cosmic origin — are presented as the root of all philosophy.

## 2. What philosophy is: the universal interest
- Contrasted with hobbies (coins, stamps, sports, horse interest) that not everyone shares.
- Philosophy asks what **should** interest everyone regardless of who/where they are: Who are we? Why are we here? How was the world created? Is there will/meaning behind events? Is there life after death? How ought we to live?
- Basic needs hierarchy: a starving person needs food, a freezing person warmth, a lonely person company. But once satisfied, philosophers claim humans cannot "live by bread alone" — everyone still needs to figure out who we are and why we are here.
- This interest is not "casual" like stamp-collecting; it is participation in a debate as old as humanity. No known culture lacks concern with what man is and where the world came from.
- There are **not many** philosophical questions, but history gives **many different answers** to each. Hence: easier to ask than to answer. Each individual must discover their own answer; encyclopedias cannot tell you whether God exists, whether there is afterlife, or how to live — but reading what others believed helps formulate your own view.
- Detective-story analogy: like Andersen vs Nielsen vs Jensen as murderer — there may be one and only one right answer (either there is afterlife or not) even if police never solve it.
- Distinction from science: some old enigmas (dark side of the moon, Man in the Moon, moon made of green cheese) were once left to imagination/discussion but are now settled by science. Philosophical questions remain open to reasoned discussion.

## 3. Wonder as the origin of philosophy
- Quote attributed to a Greek philosopher of 2000+ years ago (Socrates/Plato-Aristotle tradition): philosophy originates in man's sense of wonder — it seemed so astonishing to be alive that philosophical questions arose of their own accord.
- Magic-trick analogy: we ask how the magician turned silk scarves into a live rabbit. With the world the case is different and deeper: we know it is not mere sleight of hand because **we are in it, part of it — we are the white rabbit being pulled out of the hat.** The rabbit does not realize it is in a trick; we do, and want to know how it works.
- P.S. cosmological expansion of the image: as far as the rabbit goes, compare it to the **whole universe**; we are microscopic insects deep down in the fur. Philosophers try to climb up the fine hairs to stare straight into the magician's eyes.

## 4. THE ONLY THING REQUIRED: the faculty of wonder
- Repeated in capitals: THE ONLY THING WE REQUIRE TO BE GOOD PHILOSOPHERS IS THE FACULTY OF WONDER.
- Babies have it: after months in the womb they enter a brand-new reality, look around, reach out. Example: the "Bow-wow!" child who ecstatically shouts at every dog/elephant/hippopotamus hundreds of times while jaded adults say "All right, sit still — we've seen a dog before."
- Before the child can talk properly — long before thinking philosophically — the world becomes **habit**. "A pity, if you ask me."

## 5. Two thought experiments on habit vs wonder
- **(a) The Martian:** You walk in the woods, see a spaceship, a tiny Martian climbs out looking up at you. Striking — but have you considered **you are a Martian yourself**? You will probably never meet an alien, but you might stumble upon **yourself**: suddenly stop, see yourself in a new light — "I am an extraordinary being, a mysterious creature... stumbling around on a planet in the universe. But what is the universe?" Discovering yourself this way is as mysterious as meeting the Martian.
- **(b) Dad flying over the breakfast table:** Mom, Dad, and 2–3-year-old Thomas at breakfast. Dad floats under the ceiling while Thomas watches. Thomas just points: "Daddy's flying!" — astonished, but Dad does strange things daily (electric shaver, climbing on roof to fix aerial, black face from car engine), so one more wonder makes no difference. Mom turns, drops the jam jar, screams, may need medical attention. Why the difference? **Habit. Mom has learned people cannot fly; Thomas has not yet learned what can/can't be done.** Then the turn: what about the world itself? **The world is also floating in space.** We get used not only to gravity but to the world itself as habit, losing astonishment — losing something central that philosophers try to restore.

## 6. Children, philosophers, and the rabbit's fur
- Most people get so caught up in everyday affairs that astonishment is pushed into background — they **crawl deep into the rabbit's fur, snuggle down comfortably, and stay there for life.**
- Children: world is new, gives rise to astonishment. Adults: accept world as matter of course.
- Philosophers are the notable exception: never get quite used to the world; it continues to seem unreasonable, bewildering, enigmatic. They remain **as thin-skinned as a child** throughout life.
- Choice put to Sophie: are you a child not yet world-weary, or a philosopher who vows never to become so? If you shake your head at both, you have gotten so used to the world it no longer astonishes you — "You are on thin ice."
- Summarizing image: white rabbit pulled from top hat; trick takes **many billions of years** because rabbit is extremely large. All mortals are born at the very tip of the fine hairs, positioned to wonder at the impossibility. As they grow they work ever deeper into the fur, get comfortable, never risk crawling back up the fragile hairs. **Only philosophers embark on the perilous expedition to the outermost reaches of language and existence.** Some fall off; others cling on and yell down at the snug, food-stuffing nestlers: "Ladies and gentlemen, we are floating in space!" Reply from below: "What a bunch of troublemakers! ... Would you pass the butter, please? How much have our stocks risen? What is the price of tomatoes? Have you heard that Princess Di is expecting again?" — the trivialities of school (irregular verbs), card games, badminton vs the vital questions.
- Course terms (story frame, kept minimal): free of charge, no refund; break off by leaving a live frog / something green so the mailman won't be scared.

## Key terms
Wonder; habit; the white rabbit / top hat / magician; climbing the hairs vs crawling into the fur; "man cannot live by bread alone"; philosophical questions vs scientific questions.

## Significance / legacy (as framed by the book)
- Sets the method for the whole course: keep wonder alive; prefer your own astonished reasoning over memorized trivialities.
- Introduces the rational stance: philosophy as asking universal, un-settleable-by-encyclopedia questions and learning from history's competing answers.
