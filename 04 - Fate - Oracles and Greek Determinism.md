# 04 - Fate - Oracles and Greek Determinism

## Chapter context
After natural explanations for nature, the course turns to superstitions still governing sickness, politics, and history — grouped under fatalism.

## 1. Fatalism defined
- **Fatalism = belief that whatever happens is predestined.**
- Found all over the world, throughout history and today. Nordic version: strong belief in **"lagnadan" / fate** in old Icelandic Edda sagas.
- Greeks "great believers in fatalism" in sickness/health and political events; old superstitions had to be cleared there as they had been for natural processes.
- Modern parallels named: classmate reading magazine horoscopes; black cat = bad luck; knocking on wood; Friday the 13th, hotels with no room 13; astrology guiding even political leaders. Raises the meta-question: if Christianity/Islam counts as "faith," who gets to call astrology/Friday-13th "superstition"?

## 2. Oracles and fortune-telling: why they persist
- Belief that one's or a country's fate can be **foreseen**: cards, palm-reading, stars; Norwegian specialty — **coffee-cup grounds** (empty cup traces resemble e.g. a car → long drive for the drinker).
- The fortune-teller tries to foresee what is **really quite unforeseeable** — characteristic of all foreseeing. Because what is "seen" is so **vague**, claims are hard to repudiate.
- Stars example: sky is veritable chaos of twinkling dots, yet across ages people claim stars tell about earthly life.

## 3. The Oracle at Delphi
- Greeks consulted **Delphi** about fate. God **Apollo** spoke through priestess **Pythia**, seated on a stool over an earth fissure emitting **hypnotic vapors** that put her in trance as Apollo's mouthpiece.
- Procedure: questioners present question to oracle's **priests**, who pass it to Pythia; her answer so **obscure/ambiguous** priests must interpret it — thus people receive Apollo's all-knowing wisdom, even about the future.
- Political function: heads of state dared not go to war or take decisive steps without consulting Delphi; priests functioned as **diplomats/advisers** with intimate knowledge of peoples and countries.
- Inscription over temple entrance: **KNOW THYSELF!** — reminder man must never believe himself more than mortal and **no man can escape his destiny**.
- Literary counterpart: tragedies about people whose destiny catches up — most famous, **King Oedipus**.

## 4. History: from divine vengeance to natural explanation
- Greeks held even **world history** governed by Fate, fortunes of war swayable by gods; many today still hold God/mysterious power steers history (which, if true, removes free will — Sophie's own worry).
- Parallel to natural philosophy, first **historians** sought natural explanations: a lost war no longer acceptably explained by gods' vengeance. Named: **Herodotus (484–424 B.C.)** and **Thucydides (460–400 B.C.)**.

## 5. Medicine: from punishment of the gods to Hippocrates
- Greeks ascribed sickness to **divine intervention** — and cure via appropriate sacrifices to gods. Not uniquely Greek: pre-modern medicine widely supernatural; **"influenza" literally = malign influence from the stars**.
- Today: some call diseases (book's example: **AIDS**) God's punishment; many believe supernatural cure; many pray for recovery, implying God has power over health. Posed question: **"Is sickness the punishment of the gods?"** — Democritus, as materialist (atoms + void only), would not believe in fate.
- Concurrent with new philosophy arose Greek **medical science** seeking natural explanations. Founder: **Hippocrates**, born Cos ~460 B.C.
- Hippocratic doctrine: **moderation and healthy lifestyle** as chief safeguards; **health is the natural condition**; sickness = Nature gone off course through physical/mental imbalance; road to health = moderation, harmony, **"sound mind in a sound body."**
- **Medical ethics** rooted here: doctor must follow ethical rules — e.g., not prescribe narcotics to healthy, keep **professional secrecy** (reveal nothing of patient's illness). Required oath of pupils, quoted in full:
  - "I will follow that system or regimen which, according to my ability and judgment, I consider to be for the benefit of my patients, and abstain from whatever is deleterious and mischievous. I will give no deadly medicine to anyone if asked nor suggest any such counsel, and in like manner I will not give to a woman the means to produce an abortion. Whenever I go into a house, I will go for the benefit of the sick and will abstain from every voluntary act of mischief and corruption, and further, from the seduction of females or males, whether freemen or slaves. Whatever, in connection with my professional practice, I see or hear which ought not to be spoken abroad, I will keep secret. So long as I continue to carry out this oath unviolated, may it be granted to me to enjoy life and the practice of the art, respected by all men in all times, but should I violate this oath, may the reverse be my lot."
- Third guiding question in the chapter: **"What forces govern the course of history?"**

## Key terms
Fatalism; lagnadan; oracle; Pythia / Apollo / KNOW THYSELF; Oedipus; Herodotus / Thucydides; Hippocrates / Hippocratic oath; "sound mind in a sound body"; influenza as star-influence.

## Significance / legacy
- Extends the myth→reason move from cosmology to human affairs: prophecy, historiography, and medicine each get a naturalistic counter-project.
- Delphi shows how ambiguity + expert priests made fate socially actionable (war decisions); Hippocrates shows the ethical codification that outlives the cosmology.
